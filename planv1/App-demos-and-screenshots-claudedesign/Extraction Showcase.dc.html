Step one

State the problem, then the payoff

The dashboard is the whole setup: what the session is about, what you want out of it, and how hard you want the AI to push. Nothing is stored on a server — past sessions live in the browser.

1

Six domain tabs — Work, Home, Family, Tech, Economics, Project Planning — each loading three ready-made prompt starters.

2

Your topic, plus one-tap context keywords that append constraints like (Decision Needed) straight into the text.

3

Intention: organize an outline, force a decision, or decompress. It biases every prompt the engine writes afterwards.

4

Advanced controls: guidance tone, output filtering, and whether to run a cognitive bias audit over the finished pile.

5

Previous sessions, reopenable with their full thought history and synthesized outline.

Or skip the choosing — "Guide Me" runs a four-question warm-up diagnostic (clear or foggy · emotional or analytical · time available · decide, process, or capture) and recommends a mode with a confidence score and two alternates.

Step two · CompareSettingsModal.tsx

See what each setting does before you pick

Every control on the dashboard has an ℹ Compare affordance. It opens a six-tab guide that runs one fixed sample topic through each option, so the difference is shown rather than described.

1

Tabs for topic framing, intention, guidance tone, output filtering, bias auditing, and the warm-up quiz profiles.

2

Side-by-side columns per option, each with the interview nudge it would actually ask you.

3

And the shape of the markdown it would produce — Socratic audits read very differently from an empathetic mirror.

Step three · GuidedDrill.tsx

Guided drill — argue with the interviewer

An adaptive interview that writes its next question from your last answer. The difference from a normal chatbot: you can stop answering and interrogate the interviewer instead, then get handed back to the thread.

1

The mode tape. Switch to any of the twelve modes without losing the pile; the yellow chip counts what each mode produced.

2

A dialogue pivot: he stopped answering and asked "why does it matter who said a thing?" — the guide answered in the dashed bubble, then re-aimed the thread.

3

Toggle between answering and chatting; the mic button dictates either one via the browser's speech recognition.

4

Every exchange lands in the Surfaced Pile as an editable Q/A fragment, searchable and filterable by mode or category.

Step four · SwipeReact.tsx

Swipe deck — react instead of compose

When writing is the blocker, the engine writes candidate beliefs about your topic and you only judge them. Arrow keys work: left rejects, right resonates, down is a maybe.

1

Seven statements per deck, synthesized from the pile you already have — re-synthesize for a fresh set at any time.

2

One card at a time, deliberately large. The judgement is the work; nothing else competes for attention.

3

Swiped cards keep their verdict in the pile as STATUS: RESONANT — the synthesis weights them accordingly.

Step five · CardSort.tsx

Cluster sort — name the buckets yourself

Sorting is not busywork here: the buckets you create become the headings of the synthesized outline, so you decide the document's skeleton before the engine writes it.

1

Four buckets to start — Primary Goals, Mental Barriers, Action Tasks, Loose Details — add or delete your own.

2

Assign per fragment, with tabs to see only what's unsorted. Counts update live in the left rail.

Step six · TimelineMode.tsx

Temporal map — before, now, after

The same pile, re-read as a chronology. Origin context on the left, live pressure in the middle, forecasts and future fears on the right. Useful for separating what already happened from what you're only anticipating.

1

Three colour-keyed zones fill as you assign; an empty zone is itself a signal.

2

Assignment happens in one pass down the list below — no dragging required.

Step seven · SliderMap.tsx

Intensity map — weigh what you surfaced

Ten fragments all look equally important on a list. Rating them along four dimensions produces the heat map the synthesis uses to decide what leads the outline.

1

Pick a fragment; rated ones carry their scores back into the list as a preview.

2

Urgency, certainty, emotional charge, willingness to act — each 1 to 10, each anchored with plain-language ends.

3

High urgency plus high emotion gets flagged as a tension spot — the thing you're most likely avoiding.

Step eight · PriorityPile.tsx

Priority Eisenhower — three piles, no more

The last filter before synthesis. Act on it, watch it, or admit it's noise. Deliberately coarse: three piles force a call where a five-point scale lets you defer.

1

Zones fill as you sort; the "Leave It" pile is as informative as the other two.

2

Rank the whole pile in one downward pass. Whatever lands in "Act On This" becomes the action checklist.

This one is live — in the interactive demo you can drag a card straight into a pile, or tap Act / Watch / Leave It.

Step nine · ExportPanel.tsx

The executive blueprint

Ten scattered fragments come back as a decision, a structured outline, and a checklist — and it is re-formattable without losing the pile, so one session can produce three different documents.

1

A narrative read of where you actually landed, in the session's own words.

2

The markdown outline, viewable as rendered text or raw source, copyable in one click.

3

Re-Format Blueprint: swap tone, swap focus, toggle the bias audit, regenerate. The thought history is untouched.

4

Action items as real checkboxes, drawn from the pile you marked as actionable.

5

Print, or share via a self-contained link. Sharing is off by default — the link encodes the session in plaintext, and the UI says so.

Where the key lives

The Gemini key stays server-side behind an Express proxy at /api/session/*, rate-limited, never prefixed with VITE_ — so it is never exposed to the browser.

Where the thoughts live

Sessions persist in the browser only. Nothing is uploaded, there are no accounts, and sharing is opt-in per session.
